A Short Note About Lighthouses

A lighthouse has one job: to be seen. It does not move, it does not
speak, and it does not know who is out on the water. It simply stands
in a fixed spot and sends the same steady signal into the dark, night
after night, so that anyone who happens to be looking can find their way.

The oldest lighthouses burned wood or coal. Later ones used oil lamps
with curved mirrors behind them to throw the light farther. The real
leap came with a special lens, ground in rings, that could gather a
small flame's glow and bend it into a single bright beam.

What makes a lighthouse useful is not how clever it is, but how
reliable it is. A light that shines only sometimes is worse than no
light at all, because people learn to trust it and then it lets them
down. The same is true of many tools we build: steady and dependable
beats flashy and unpredictable.
